Most matcha sold outside Japan is blended. Leaf from several regions is combined to hit a consistent flavour and a consistent price, year after year. This is not dishonest — blending is a genuine craft, and a well-built blend is stable in a way single-origin never is.

But it averages everything out. The particular character of a place is exactly what gets smoothed away.

Keeping a garden separate costs more, yields less, and produces a tea that changes from season to season. What you get in return is the ability to taste where something came from.

## Uji, Kyoto

Matcha cultivation in Japan effectively begins here. Tea has been grown in the hills south of Kyoto since the twelfth century, and the techniques that define matcha — shading, stone milling, stripping stem from leaf — were refined in this valley over centuries.

The land shows up in the cup. River fog settles in the valleys and acts as natural shade; the soil is heavy and mineral. Uji matcha tends toward deep umami with a long vegetal sweetness and very little edge — the flavour arrives slowly and stays. It is the reference point most people are measuring other matcha against, whether or not they know it.

## Nishio, Aichi

Nishio sits on the Yahagi river, on flat alluvial ground that floods and enriches. It produces a large share of Japan’s total matcha, and its scale has given it a reputation for consistency rather than romance — which undersells the tea.

The character here is brighter and more forward than Uji. Less of the low, savoury depth; more immediate sweetness and a cleaner, lighter finish. It is the most approachable of the three, and often the one people prefer without being able to say why. If Uji is a slow arrival, Nishio is a clear one.

## Yame, Fukuoka

Yame is further south, in Kyushu, in narrow mountain valleys with sharp swings between day and night temperature. Those swings stress the plant, and stressed plants concentrate.

The result is the boldest of the three: deeply mineral, structured, noticeably less sweet. Yame rewards attention and punishes carelessness — brewed a few degrees too hot it turns austere. Drinkers who find other matcha too soft tend to end up here and stay.

## What this means for a bowl

Taste them side by side, prepared identically, and the differences are not subtle. Same grade, same water, same technique, three distinctly different cups.

Which is better is not a real question. Uji for depth, Nishio for brightness, Yame for structure — the useful question is which one suits how you drink and what time of day you drink it. Many people keep two, and reach for a different one in the morning than in the afternoon.

## A note on vintage

Single-origin means single harvest as well as single place. A garden that produced an exceptional tea one May may produce merely good tea the next, depending on rainfall, temperature, and when the shading went up.

This is the trade. Blends protect you from variation; single-origin exposes you to it. Some years the tea is remarkable. That is the point of keeping it separate.
