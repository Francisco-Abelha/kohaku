Neither term is regulated. There is no Japanese authority certifying a matcha as "ceremonial," and no threshold a powder must cross to be sold as one. The words describe intended use, and they are only as reliable as the person using them.

That said, they point at real differences. Understanding what actually varies is more useful than trusting the label.

## What actually changes

**Leaf age.** The first harvest of the year, picked in May, produces the youngest and most tender leaves. These have spent the longest under shade, developed the most amino acids, and contain the least fibre. Later harvests — second, third, sometimes fourth — give progressively coarser leaves with more catechins and more bitterness. Grade tracks harvest more closely than it tracks anything else.

**Shading time.** Matcha plants are covered for roughly three to four weeks before picking. Denying the plant light forces it to overproduce chlorophyll and L-theanine, which is where both the colour and the savoury sweetness come from. Shorter shading means a duller, sharper tea.

**What gets removed.** For the highest grades, stems and veins are stripped out entirely before milling, leaving only leaf flesh. Cheaper powders include more of the whole leaf, which adds fibre, bitterness, and a browner cast.

**How it is ground.** Stone mills turn slowly enough not to heat the leaf — around thirty grams an hour. Heat degrades aroma. Industrial grinders are faster and cheaper and produce a coarser, flatter powder.

## How to tell without the label

Colour is the most honest signal. High-grade matcha is vivid and slightly blue-green. As grade drops, the colour moves toward olive, then toward yellow-brown.

Texture is second. Rub a pinch between your fingers: good matcha feels like eyeshadow or talc, not like ground herbs. Coarse powder will not suspend properly and will settle within a minute of whisking.

Smell is third. Fresh, high-grade matcha smells sweet and marine — something like nori. Older or lower grades smell hay-like, or of nothing much at all.

## Which to use

Drink the high grades straight. Whisked with water and nothing else, a ceremonial matcha has enough sweetness and body to stand alone, and any bitterness is a fault rather than a feature. Paying for that quality and then burying it under milk and syrup is a waste — you will not taste the difference you paid for.

Use lower grades where something else is doing the heavy lifting. In a latte, the milk’s fat and sugar round off exactly the astringency that would ruin a straight bowl. In baking, sugar and heat do the same. A powder that is unpleasant on its own can be very good in a cake.

Between those poles sits everyday matcha: good enough to drink straight on a weekday, robust enough not to disappear into oat milk. For most people, most days, this is the honest answer.

## On price

Expect to pay meaningfully more per gram for first-harvest, stone-milled, single-origin matcha, and expect that price to be justified only if you drink it straight and drink it fresh. A superb matcha left open in a cupboard for three months is worse than an ordinary one opened yesterday.

Buy the grade that matches how you actually drink, and buy less of it more often.
